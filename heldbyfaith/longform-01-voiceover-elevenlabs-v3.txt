HELD BY FAITH — LONG-FORM 01
"They Told Me The Cancer Was Gone. Nobody Warned Me What Came After."
VOICEOVER SCRIPT — ELEVENLABS v3, EMOTION-TAGGED
==============================================================================

HOW TO RUN THIS
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
• Generate ONE BATCH AT A TIME, in order. Each batch below is a separate generation.
  v3 gets unstable over very long inputs and drifts in tone between paragraphs;
  batching by act keeps the delivery consistent inside each emotional register.
• Every batch is well over 250 characters, which is what v3 needs to settle. Do not
  split them smaller.
• SETTINGS: Stability LOW-to-NATURAL. Low gives v3 room to act the tags; if it starts
  over-performing the grief, move to Natural. Never High — it flattens the tags out.
• Speed: slightly below default. This script is ~1,510 words over ~13:00, so roughly
  40% of the runtime is silence. The tags do some of that; the edit does the rest.
• The [pause] and [long pause] tags are deliberate and load-bearing. Do not remove
  them to make the read flow better — the flow is not supposed to be smooth.

THE ONE RULE FOR THIS CHANNEL
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Do not perform the grief. Report it. The channel's like rate is what it is because the
delivery is flat and honest, not because it is moving. If a take sounds like it is
trying to make you feel something, it is wrong. Regenerate it.

⚠️ SQUARE BRACKETS ARE AUDIO TAGS IN v3.
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Every [tag] below is intended for the model. The lines only you can write are marked
with >>>>>> ARROWS <<<<<< instead of brackets, precisely so v3 cannot mistake them for
tags. Replace those blocks with your own words BEFORE generating. If you generate with
the arrow blocks still in, v3 will read them aloud.

BATCH 1 — COLD OPEN — the drawer   [S01–S08]
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

[softly] There's a drawer in my kitchen I open most mornings.
There's a hospital wristband in it. Cut off, kept.
[quietly] I don't look at it for comfort. I look at it to check something.
Eleven months ago a doctor said the words I had spent a year praying for. He said: we think it's gone.
[thoughtful] And I want to tell you honestly [pause] what that felt like. Because it is not what I expected, and nobody warned me.
[flat] I walked out to the car park and I felt nothing at all.
I had built a whole year around one sentence, and when it came I couldn't feel it.
[softly] This video is about the part after. The part I couldn't find anywhere when I went looking.

BATCH 2 — ACT 1 — the anticlimax   [S09–S16]
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Everybody around me was celebrating. That was the strangest week of the whole thing.
[quietly] They had waited a year to be happy for me and I could not join in. I performed it. I was good at it by then.
That night I cut the wristband off and I didn't throw it away.
[thoughtful] I have thought about why for eleven months. I'll tell you at the end, because it took me the whole story to work it out.
[measured] Here's the thing nobody says. Being told you're clear doesn't end it. [pause] It just takes away the thing you were allowed to be scared of.
For a year I had a job. Get through it. Then the job ended and I still couldn't eat a plate of rice in under an hour.
The cancer was gone. The body it happened to was still mine.
[flat] And nobody claps for that part. There's no appointment where they tell you the rest of it is over.

After a Whipple, your body no longer does something it has always done for you without asking.
[tired] So eating stopped being a thing I did and became a thing I managed. An hour, for a plate of rice.

[hesitant] Knowing it's allowed and feeling it are different rooms. I knew I wasn't sinning. I still felt like I was watching my own religion from a chair.

Thirty years of my life had a shape and that year it didn't. I ate at noon and felt like a stranger in my own house.
[warmly] Here's what I'd say to you if you're there now. Your deen did not get smaller. Your body did.

BATCH 4 — ACT 3 — the ward   [S29–S37]
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

I want to take you into the room, because I think people picture it wrong.
[measured] Twenty-two chairs. All full. And nobody asks anybody what they've got.
[quietly] You'd think it would be the one place you could. It's the one place you don't. It's the only unspoken rule and everybody keeps it.
You talk about parking. About the weather. About the coffee machine on the second floor.
[flat] And then one week a chair is empty and nobody says anything about that either.
The week I was diagnosed I googled things I will not repeat here. You know the ones. Survival, and then a number, and then a year.
[firm] Don't. Or do, because you're going to anyway. But know that the number you find is old, and it isn't you.
And nobody warns you about the paperwork. I spent more hours on forms than in surgery.
[a small laugh] There is something almost funny about that. I laughed at it once and it turned into something else halfway through.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------

[softly] There was a man in the chair next to mine. >>> HIS NAME <<<

[quietly] He was doing better than me. Everyone said so. He was doing better than me right up until he wasn't. [long pause]
[flat] I found out the way you find out. Somebody didn't say his name.
And then eleven months later a doctor told me mine was gone.
[quietly] Nobody prepares you for what that does. You are not allowed to be unhappy about good news.
[hesitant] So you say Alhamdulillah, and you mean it, and underneath it you are asking a question you know you shouldn't ask.
[almost a whisper] Why him and not me. [long pause] There is no good answer to that and I have stopped looking for one.
[softly] What I do instead is say his name out loud sometimes. That's all I've got. It turns out that's allowed to be all you've got.

BATCH 6 — ACT 5 — 3am, and what sabr means   [S48–S59]
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

[quietly] Which brings me to a night about four months in. Three in the morning, second night after surgery.
I still had the wristband on then.
[whispers] And I asked Allah, out loud, in an empty room, what the pain was for.
[thoughtful] Not why. [pause] What for. There's a difference and I only found it that night.
Why is a complaint dressed as a question. It wants somebody to be at fault.
What for is a different thing entirely. It assumes there's a use. It's the harder question because it puts some of it back on you.
[plainly] I didn't get an answer that night. I want to be very clear about that. Nothing happened. [pause] No light, no dream, nothing.
But I stopped asking the first question. And about a month later I noticed I had been getting an answer the whole time, just not in words.
[measured] Everyone had told me to have sabr. I had always heard that as: endure this quietly and don't complain.
[warmly] That isn't what it is. Sabr isn't going quiet. It's staying — staying in the room, staying in the relationship with Allah — while it hurts.
You are allowed to say it hurts. Yaqub said it hurts. He said it out loud and it's in the Quran.
[warmly] What you're not asked to do is leave. [pause] That's the whole thing. Sabr is a location, not a volume.

BATCH 7 — ACT 6 — the wristband   [S60–S68]
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Two quick things, and then the drawer.
[firm] One. If someone you love is sick, please stop saying 'everything happens for a reason.' Say 'I'm here Thursday.' Give them a day, not a philosophy.
[softly] Somebody paid my rent one month and never told me who. That person understood something the rest of us are still working out.
Two. People ask what cancer took. It took a year, an organ, and [sighs] a version of me who thought he had time to waste.
[quietly] What it gave back is harder to say out loud without sounding like a poster. So I'll say it as plainly as I can: I am here now. Actually here. I wasn't before.
So. The drawer. I open it most mornings and I look at a cut wristband, and I'm not being sentimental. I'm checking a date.
[softly] Because a year ago I would have told you I wanted more time. I had it. [pause] I just wasn't in it.
[warmly] The wristband is how I remember to actually turn up to my own life. Then I close the drawer and go and have breakfast.
[warmly] If you're the one who just got the phone call, there are shorter pieces of this on the channel. Start wherever it hurts. I'll see you there.
