## SCENE 4 — WHAT A MORTGAGE ACTUALLY IS
**1:20 – 2:50 · 90s**

**Visual:**
- 1:20 — Two boxes: BANK and YOU. Arrow of cash from bank to you. Then a second arrow: you → seller. House icon moves to your side.
- 1:50 — A dotted "charge" line locks from the house back to the bank. Not ownership — a claim.
- 2:15 — Interest arrow loops from you back to the bank, thick and continuous.

**On-screen text:** `LOAN ≠ SALE` · `THE BANK NEVER OWNS THE HOUSE`

**VO:**
> Here's the part that gets skipped. When you take a conventional mortgage, the bank does
> not buy you a house. The bank lends you cash. You buy the house. The bank takes a legal
> charge over it as security.
>
> That distinction is everything. The bank never owns the property. It never carries the
> risk of owning it. If the roof fails, that's yours. If the market collapses, that's
> yours. If the building burns down uninsured, you still owe every penny.
>
> What the bank owns is a claim on you. And that claim grows at a fixed percentage,
> guaranteed, regardless of what happens to the asset or to you. Money lent, more money
> back, no ownership and no risk taken in between.
>
> That is the definition. Not the marketing — the mechanism.

**B-roll:** Land Registry documents, keys on a table, a surveyor's report. Keep it cool
and neutral; no doom imagery.

## SCENE 5 — WHY THE EARLY YEARS ARE BRUTAL
**2:50 – 4:20 · 90s**

**Visual:** Full-width amortisation chart. Two stacked areas — red interest, green
principal — animating left to right across 25 years. Playhead moves slowly through year
1, then accelerates. Callout at year 10 marking the crossover point.

**On-screen text:** `YEAR 1: 71% INTEREST` · `CROSSOVER: YEAR 13`

**VO:**
> Now watch how it's sequenced. Interest is charged on what you still owe. At the start
> you owe almost everything, so almost everything you pay is interest.
>
> In year one, seventeen and a half thousand pounds leaves your account. Twelve thousand
> three hundred of it is interest. Seventy-one percent. You are not really buying a house
> in year one. You are renting the money to buy it later.
>
> The crossover — the first month where more of your payment goes to the house than to
> the bank — doesn't arrive until year thirteen. Half the term. And that's if rates never
> move and you never remortgage. Every time you remortgage, the clock resets and you walk
> back into the front-loaded years again.

**Retention note:** The chart is the whole scene. Let it breathe — do not cut away.

## SCENE 6 — THE PRINCIPLE
**4:20 – 5:20 · 60s**

**Visual:** Restrained typography. Qur'an 2:275 in Arabic, translation beneath. No
ornament, no nasheed, no glow. Then a simple two-column card: PERMITTED / FORBIDDEN.

**On-screen text:** `"Allah has permitted trade and forbidden riba."` — al-Baqarah 2:275
Then: `TRADE: own it, risk it, profit` / `RIBA: lend it, risk nothing, profit`

**VO:**
> This is where the Qur'anic ruling stops being abstract. The verse doesn't forbid profit.
> It permits trade and forbids riba — in the same breath, in the same sentence.
>
> So the line isn't between making money and not making money. It's between two ways of
> making it. In trade, you own something, you carry the risk of owning it, and you profit
> if it goes well. In riba, you part with money, you carry no risk at all, and your return
> is guaranteed in advance.
>
> Ownership and risk. Hold onto those two words, because every structure in the next
> half of this video is an attempt to put them back into the transaction.

**Retention note:** Deliver this straight and unadorned. The restraint is what makes it
land — and it separates you from every channel that scores this moment with reverb.

## SCENE 7 — STRUCTURE 1: MURABAHA
**5:20 – 7:00 · 100s**

**Visual:** Three-step animation, each step snapping into place.
1. Bank → seller: bank buys the house. House icon moves to BANK.
2. House sits on the bank's side. Brief hold — this is the ownership moment.
3. Bank → you: bank sells at a disclosed price. House moves to YOU. Payment schedule appears, every instalment identical.

**On-screen text:** `MURABAHA` · `cost + disclosed markup` · `PRICE FIXED AT SIGNING`

**VO:**
> Structure one: Murabaha. Cost-plus sale.
>
> The bank buys the property outright. It actually owns it — title, risk, the lot. Then it
> sells that property on to you at a price that's stated openly: the cost, plus an agreed
> markup. You pay that total in instalments over an agreed term.
>
> Two things follow from that. The bank took ownership, however briefly, and with it the
> risk. And your price is fixed at signature. Two hundred and ninety thousand pounds is
> two hundred and ninety thousand pounds — it cannot grow. There is no compounding,
> because there's no outstanding loan to compound. There's a sale price and a balance
> remaining on it.
>
> The trade-off is rigidity. Fixed price cuts both ways: settle early and you may not save
> much, because you owe a price, not an interest-bearing balance.

---

## SCENE 8 — STRUCTURE 2: IJARA WA IQTINA
**7:00 – 8:45 · 105s**

**Visual:** Bank buys house (same visual language as Scene 7 — reuse deliberately). Then
a lease document animates between BANK and YOU. A progress bar labelled OWNERSHIP fills
month by month. At 100% the deed icon flips from bank-side to your-side.

**On-screen text:** `IJARA WA IQTINA` · `lease ending in ownership` · `TITLE TRANSFERS AT TERM`

**VO:**
> Structure two: Ijara wa Iqtina — a lease that ends in ownership.
>
> Again the bank buys the property and holds the title. But this time it doesn't sell it
> to you up front. It leases it to you. You pay rent for the use of an asset that someone
> else owns — which is an ordinary, permitted transaction. At the end of the term, or
> progressively along the way, ownership transfers to you.
>
> Because the bank holds the title, it holds the obligations that come with it. Structural
> insurance, major repairs, and the loss if the building is destroyed — those sit with the
> owner, not the tenant. That's not a technicality. That's the risk the whole ruling turns
> on, sitting where it should.
>
> The trade-off is that rent can be reviewed. A poorly written Ijara lets the bank reprice
> the rent against a market benchmark — and if that benchmark is just the base rate under
> a different name, you have re-created the thing you were avoiding. Which brings us to
> the structure most British and American Islamic banks actually use.

**Retention note:** That last sentence is the bridge. Do not cut to a chapter card here —
run straight into Scene 9 so the momentum carries.

---

## SCENE 9 — STRUCTURE 3: DIMINISHING MUSHARAKA
**8:45 – 10:30 · 105s**

**Visual:** A single horizontal bar representing the property. Split 10% YOU (green) /
90% BANK (grey). Two arrows each month: one labelled RENT (on the bank's share only), one
labelled BUY-OUT (moving the boundary). The green section grows across the timeline. The
rent arrow visibly thins as the grey shrinks.

**On-screen text:** `DIMINISHING MUSHARAKA` · `co-ownership` · `RENT FALLS AS YOUR SHARE GROWS`

**VO:**
> Structure three: Diminishing Musharaka. Shared, shrinking partnership. This is what
> Al Rayan, Gatehouse and Guidance Residential are built on.
>
> You and the bank buy the property together. You put in ten percent, the bank puts in
> ninety. You are co-owners, on the title, in those proportions.
>
> Each month you make two payments that are deliberately kept separate. The first is rent
> — but only on the share you don't own yet. The second buys a slice of the bank's share
> and moves it to your side permanently.
>
> And here's the part worth sitting with. As your share grows, the bank's share shrinks —
> so the rent you owe on it falls, every single month, automatically. Your position
> improves as time passes rather than in spite of it. Compare that to the amortisation
> chart from earlier, where you were still underwater at year ten.
>
> Ownership is real, it's on the register, and the risk is shared in proportion to it.

**Retention note:** Explicitly call back to the year-10 chart. Closing the loop you opened
at 0:18 is what holds people to the end.

## SCENE 10 — SIDE BY SIDE
**10:30 – 11:15 · 45s**

**Visual:** Four-column comparison grid building row by row. Rows appear in sync with VO;
never show the finished table before the VO reaches it.

| | Conventional | Murabaha | Ijara | Musharaka |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bank owns property | ✗ | ✓ (briefly) | ✓ (throughout) | ✓ (shared) |
| Total cost fixed | ✗ | ✓ | ✗ (reviewable) | ✗ (reviewable) |
| Cost falls over time | ✗ | ✗ | ✗ | ✓ |
| Early settlement | Saves interest | Limited benefit | Flexible | Flexible |
| Who bears total loss | You | Bank pre-sale | Bank | Both, pro rata |

**VO:**
> All three side by side. Murabaha gives you certainty — a fixed price you can plan
> around. Ijara gives you flexibility and puts the ownership burden squarely on the bank.
> Musharaka gives you a cost that falls as you go, and it's the one most widely available
> in the West. None of them is automatically the best. They answer different questions.

## SCENE 11 — THE FOUR QUESTIONS
**11:15 – 12:30 · 75s**

**Visual:** Checklist on a clean card, each item ticking on as it's spoken. Question 4
appears in a different accent colour and holds on screen for 5 seconds at the end.

**On-screen text:**
`1. Does the bank take legal title?`
`2. Is the profit disclosed and fixed at signing?`
`3. Who pays if the property is destroyed?`
`4. What happens if you pay late — and where does that money go?`

**VO:**
> Now the part that matters most, because not every product marketed as Islamic survives
> contact with these four questions.
>
> One. Does the bank actually take legal title, even for a moment? If it never appears on
> the register, you have a loan with a different name on the paperwork.
>
> Two. Is the profit disclosed and agreed at signing, or does it float against a benchmark?
> Floating isn't automatically fatal — but if it tracks the base rate exactly, ask what
> the structure is really doing.
>
> Three. If the property is destroyed and uninsured, who takes the loss? If the answer is
> "you, entirely," then the bank never took ownership risk, whatever the contract is
> called.
>
> Four — the one almost nobody asks. What happens if you pay late? In a genuine Islamic
> contract a late charge cannot become profit for the bank, because profit on delay is
> exactly riba. It must go to charity, and the bank must be able to show you that it does.
> Ask them. The answer tells you more than the brochure ever will.
>
> And I'll be honest with you: there are serious scholars who argue some of these products
> are conventional lending with extra steps. That criticism isn't unreasonable. These four
> questions are how you check a specific offer instead of trusting a label.

**Retention note:** The honesty beat is deliberate. In this niche, acknowledging the
critique buys more credibility than defending the industry — and it's the comment-driver.
