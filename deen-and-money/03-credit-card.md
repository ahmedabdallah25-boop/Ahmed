## SCRIPT

### ACT 0 — COLD OPEN (0:00–0:35)

> Say you owe five thousand dollars on a credit card. And say you do everything right. You pay the
> minimum every single month. On time. Never late, never missed, not once, for as long as it takes.
>
> It will take nineteen years.
>
> You will hand over thirteen thousand, one hundred dollars to clear a five thousand dollar debt.
> Eight thousand, one hundred dollars of that is the cost of having waited.
>
> And I want to be precise about something, because it changes how you should feel about this.
> That is not the punishment for being irresponsible. That is the outcome you get for following the
> instructions printed on the statement.

### ACT 1 — THE PROMISE (0:35–1:15)

> In the next twelve minutes I'm going to show you the exact formula behind the minimum payment,
> why it produces nineteen years, and one specific change — fifty dollars — that cuts sixteen years
> off it.
>
> Two things to watch for. There's a number hiding in your first payment that I think will genuinely
> annoy you when you see it. And there's a question I want you holding the whole way through: when
> you're struggling to pay, what happens to the price? Because the answer to that one question tells
> you everything about what kind of contract you actually signed — and at the end I'll show you a
> system where the answer runs in exactly the opposite direction.

### ACT 2 — THE NORMAL CASE (1:15–3:20)

> First, how the balance got there. Because there's a story people tell about credit card debt, and
> it's mostly wrong.
>
> The story is that it's a discipline problem — that people put televisions and holidays on cards
> they can't afford. That happens. It is not the main event. Look at what actually puts people into
> revolving debt and you find a car that failed its inspection, a medical bill, a deposit on a new
> flat, a gap between jobs, a boiler in February. Ordinary life arriving with a number attached at a
> moment when there wasn't a number available.
>
> So the balance appears. Five thousand dollars. And now the statement arrives.
>
> On that statement, in small type, is a line called the minimum payment due. And here is the thing
> I want you to notice about that line: it is *formatted like advice*. It sits there in the same
> font as your account number and your due date, presented as neutral information — this is the
> amount you need to pay. Everything about the design says *this is the responsible number*.
>
> It isn't information. It's a setting. Somebody chose it. And what they chose is a formula.
>
> The common one works like this: **one percent of your balance, plus all of that month's interest.**
> With a floor of about twenty-five dollars so it never gets too small to bother collecting.
>
> Read that formula again, because it's doing something very specific. It covers all of the interest
> — every cent of it, so the balance can never grow — and then it takes exactly one percent off the
> debt itself.
>
> One percent. That's the setting. The minimum payment is engineered to reduce what you owe by one
> percent a month, and no faster.
>
> Let's watch what one percent a month does.

### ACT 3 — THE BUILD (3:20–6:20)

> Twenty-two percent a year is one point eight three percent a month. On five thousand dollars,
> that's ninety-one dollars and sixty-seven cents of interest in month one.
>
> So your minimum payment is fifty dollars — that's the one percent — plus ninety-one sixty-seven.
> **A hundred and forty-one dollars, sixty-seven cents.**
>
> Here's the number I promised would annoy you. Of that payment, **sixty-five percent is interest.**
> You hand over a hundred and forty-two dollars and fifty of it touches the debt.
>
> Now month two. Balance is four thousand nine hundred and fifty. And because the minimum is a
> *percentage* of the balance, the minimum drops too. Your required payment goes down.
>
> That's the mechanism. Most people never see it because it happens by three dollars at a time. The
> lower your balance gets, the less you're asked to pay — which means the balance falls more slowly
> — which means it takes longer. The minimum payment is a curve that flattens as it approaches zero
> and gets asymptotically polite about it.
>
> After **one full year** of never missing a payment, you've paid about sixteen hundred and fifty
> dollars. Your balance has gone from five thousand to four thousand four hundred and thirty.
> Sixteen hundred and fifty dollars spent to remove five hundred and seventy dollars of debt.
>
> **Year five:** you've paid roughly six thousand eight hundred. You still owe about two thousand
> seven hundred.
>
> **Year ten:** you've paid roughly nine thousand nine hundred. You still owe about fifteen hundred.
>
> You are ten years in. You have paid double what you borrowed. You still owe a third of it.
>
> Eventually the required payment slides down to the twenty-five dollar floor, and from there it's a
> long flat crawl. The last payment lands in **year nineteen, month two.**
>
> Total: **thirteen thousand, one hundred dollars.**
>
> Now. The fifty dollars.
>
> Take that first minimum — a hundred and forty-one sixty-seven — and round it up to a hundred and
> ninety-two. Fifty dollars more. One takeaway meal a month.
>
> And then here's the actual trick, and it's the whole trick: **don't let it fall.** Pay a hundred
> and ninety-two every month, fixed, regardless of what the statement asks for. You are refusing the
> curve.
>
> That debt is gone in **three years.** Total paid: **six thousand, eight hundred and sixty-two
> dollars.**
>
> Same card. Same rate. Same debt. Sixteen years and six thousand two hundred and forty dollars,
> for fifty dollars a month and one decision — to ignore the number they printed for you.

### ACT 4 — THE RE-HOOK (6:20–6:50)

> Now, I could stop here and this would be a useful video. Pay more than the minimum, fix the
> payment, done.
>
> But that would let the important part off the hook. Because that nineteen-year outcome isn't a
> flaw. Nobody at the card company is embarrassed about it. A customer who pays the minimum forever
> is not a failure of the product — that customer *is* the product, and there's an industry term for
> them: a revolver. The profitable one.
>
> Before we get into what that means, the objection.

### ACT 5 — THE OBJECTION (6:50–8:20)

> "This is a self-inflicted problem. Don't carry a balance. Pay it in full every month and the card
> costs you nothing — plus you get rewards and you build a credit history. The tool is fine; the
> user is the problem."
>
> Let me give that its due, because a lot of it is correct.
>
> If you clear the full statement balance every month, you genuinely pay zero interest. The grace
> period is real. The cashback is real. The credit file is real, and in most countries a thin credit
> file costs you money on everything from a mortgage to a phone contract. For a person who never
> revolves, a credit card is a genuinely good deal and I'm not going to pretend otherwise.
>
> Here's what that argument misses.
>
> The rewards you get are funded by somebody. Interchange fees pay some of it. The rest is paid by
> the people carrying balances at twenty-two percent. The convenience user's cashback is subsidised
> by the revolver's interest — the person with the least money in the arrangement is transferring
> value to the person with the most. That's the design, and "just don't be that person" is advice
> that only works for people who were never going to be that person.
>
> And the sharper point: nobody plans to revolve. Everyone in year nine of a five-thousand-dollar
> balance opened that card expecting to clear it monthly. The product does not require you to make
> a bad decision. It only requires one bad month.
>
> So no — I don't think the tool is neutral. A contract where the safest available behaviour, done
> perfectly, for nineteen years, produces that outcome, isn't a neutral tool with careless users.
> It's a designed result.

### ACT 6 — THE TURN (8:20–9:00)

> So what is actually being sold here?
>
> Not money. You already had access to the money — that's what the credit line is. Not a product,
> not a service, not risk-bearing: if you default they sell the debt to a collector and move on.
>
> They're selling **delay.** And the price of the delay is set so that the delay is nearly permanent.
>
> Now hold that next to the question I gave you at the start. When you're struggling — when the
> month goes badly and you can't pay — what happens to the price?
>
> It goes *up*. That's what a penalty rate is. The worse your position, the more you're charged; the
> longer you take, the more they earn. The lender's income is inversely tied to your recovery.
>
> That's not a moral complaint about anyone at the bank. It's a structural fact about the contract.
> And it's precisely the structure that one legal tradition looked at and decided could not exist.

### ACT 7 — THE DIVE (9:00–11:30)

> Islamic finance doesn't try to reform this contract. It deletes the category.
>
> Lending money at a price is *riba*, and it's prohibited outright. Not capped at a reasonable rate,
> not restricted to responsible lenders — prohibited. Charging more for money because time passed
> is not a transaction that's allowed to happen.
>
> Which raises the obvious question: so is lending just banned? Are you supposed to refuse when
> someone needs five thousand dollars?
>
> No. Lending is *encouraged*. It's just lending, rather than selling.
>
> The permitted form is **qard hasan** — a good loan, a benevolent loan. You lend a thousand, you
> get back a thousand. Not a cent more, ever, no matter how long it takes. That's the whole contract.
>
> And people's first reaction is: then why would anyone do it? Which, I'd point out, is a very
> revealing question. We've become so used to money being a rental item that a loan with no return
> sounds economically irrational rather than, you know, *what a loan is*. Qard hasan is treated as
> one of the most meritorious things you can do with wealth — the entire upside is non-financial.
>
> Now, the part I actually want you to take away, because it's not the prohibition. It's what the
> tradition says the lender must do **when the borrower is in trouble.**
>
> The Qur'an addresses this directly. If the debtor is in difficulty, grant them time until things
> become easy — and if you write the debt off as charity, that's better for you. Verse two-eighty.
>
> Read that against what we just built.
>
> The credit card's answer to difficulty is a penalty rate. Miss a payment, and the price of your
> difficulty increases. The system is arranged so that your worst month is the lender's best one.
>
> The other answer to difficulty is: stop the clock, extend the time, and consider forgiving it.
>
> Those aren't two versions of the same idea with different branding. They point in opposite
> directions. One treats your hardship as an input; the other treats it as a reason to stand down.
> That's what the prohibition is actually protecting — not a technicality about percentages, but the
> possibility of a debt that doesn't grow while you're drowning.
>
> **What to do on a Monday morning, in order.**
>
> One: stop new spending on the card. Nothing else works while the balance is still being fed.
>
> Two: fix your payment. Pick a number above the minimum and never let it fall, even as the
> statement asks for less. This one decision does most of the work.
>
> Three: pay the highest rate first — every spare dollar to the most expensive balance while others
> get their fixed payment. It clears faster and costs less than any other order.
>
> Four: a zero percent balance transfer can be genuinely useful, but read what it is. There's
> usually a three-to-four percent fee up front, and the promotional period ends — and if there's a
> balance left when it does, the rate that replaces it is often worse than the one you left. It's a
> deadline, not a rescue. Only take it with a written plan to clear it inside the window.
>
> Five: if you're in real difficulty, call them before you miss a payment, not after. Hardship
> programmes exist and are underused — reduced rates, frozen interest, restructured terms. They
> won't offer; you have to ask.
>
> And if you're in a position to be on the other side of this — a family member, a friend, a
> colleague quietly drowning — qard hasan is available to you right now. It doesn't require an
> institution. It requires lending someone five thousand dollars and asking for five thousand back.

### ACT 8 — THE CLOSE (11:30–12:10)

> Five thousand dollars. Nineteen years. Thirteen thousand, one hundred dollars paid. Sixty-five
> percent of your first payment gone before it touched the debt.
>
> And fifty dollars a month, held steady, takes sixteen years off it.
>
> The single most valuable thing you can do after this video is open your statement, find the
> minimum, and decide — right now, once — what you're going to pay instead. Then never look at that
> line again.
>
> Next time: the biggest number in the series. A four hundred thousand dollar house, eight hundred
> and eight thousand dollars out of your pocket, and nineteen years before a single payment is
> mostly yours.
>
> Run the numbers. Then run them halal.
