## SCRIPT

### ACT 0 — COLD OPEN (0:00–0:35)

> There are two cars in your driveway right now. You can only see one of them.
>
> You're paying for both.
>
> The one you can see cost twenty-eight thousand dollars — that's the number on the window, the
> number you negotiated, the number you told your family. The one you can't see costs eight thousand,
> three hundred and forty dollars. Nobody delivers it. You can't drive it, sit in it, insure it, or
> sell it. It doesn't exist. And you will make payments on it for six years.
>
> In the next eleven minutes I'm going to show you exactly where that second car comes from — and
> the way it's been removed from the deal for about fourteen hundred years.

### ACT 1 — THE PROMISE (0:35–1:15)

> By the end of this you'll be able to work out the real price of any car you're ever offered, in
> about thirty seconds, in your head, before the salesperson finishes the sentence.
>
> Two things to watch for. There's a stretch of this loan — and it's longer than you'd expect —
> where if you sold the car and handed over every cent, you would still owe money. I'll tell you
> exactly how long that stretch lasts.
>
> And second: at some point you have to answer a very specific question about what the lender
> actually sold you. Not the car — the dealer sold you the car. Something else. When you can name
> it, the whole thing looks different, and so does every other loan you'll ever sign.

### ACT 2 — THE NORMAL CASE (1:15–3:15)

> Let's do this the way it really happens.
>
> Twenty-eight thousand dollars. You negotiated. You got them down. You walked out feeling like you
> won something, and honestly — on the price of the car, you probably did.
>
> Then you sat in the second office. The little one, off to the side, after the handshake. And in
> that room nobody talks about twenty-eight thousand dollars any more. In that room there's exactly
> one number: **five hundred and five dollars a month.**
>
> That's not an accident. That's the product being sold, and it's sold that way because five hundred
> and five is a number you can imagine surviving. Twenty-eight thousand isn't. One of those numbers
> makes you cautious and the other one makes you feel capable.
>
> So watch what happens to the conversation. It stops being *how much does this car cost* and becomes
> *how much can you handle each month*. And once that's the question, the answer is always the same:
> a longer term. Because a longer term makes the monthly number smaller, and a smaller monthly number
> feels like a discount.
>
> It isn't a discount. Six years at nine percent — that's the deal on the table. Seventy-two
> payments of five hundred and four dollars and seventy-two cents.
>
> Now, everyone can multiply. Almost nobody does. So let's do it.

### ACT 3 — THE BUILD (3:15–6:00)

> Five hundred and four seventy-two, times seventy-two months.
>
> **Thirty-six thousand, three hundred and forty dollars.**
>
> On a twenty-eight thousand dollar car.
>
> Eight thousand, three hundred and forty dollars of that never touched the vehicle. It didn't go to
> the factory that built it. It didn't go to the dealer who sold it. It didn't pay for a single bolt,
> a single litre of fuel, or a single service. It bought you nothing that exists in the physical
> world.
>
> That's a used car. A decent one. Sitting in someone else's driveway.
>
> Now let's look at where it goes inside the loan, because the shape matters. In month one, your
> five hundred and five dollars splits like this: two hundred and ten dollars is the cost of the
> finance, and two hundred and ninety-five actually reduces what you owe. So in your first month of
> ownership, forty-two percent of your payment bought you nothing.
>
> That ratio improves as you go, which is the one genuinely fair thing about how these are
> structured. By the final year, almost all of it is going to the car. But you paid the expensive
> part first — and that's the part people don't plan for, because it interacts with the second
> thing that's happening to your car.
>
> Which is that it's falling apart in value while you pay for it.
>
> Drive it off the lot and a twenty-eight thousand dollar car is worth about twenty-four thousand.
> Not because anything happened to it — because it's now used. Your loan balance at that moment is
> still twenty-eight thousand.
>
> **You are four thousand dollars underwater before you reach the end of the road.**
>
> And you stay underwater. The car loses value faster than the loan shrinks for the first three
> years and change. Roughly forty months in, they finally cross. Which means for over half the term
> of that loan, if you sold the car and handed the dealership every dollar you got — you'd still owe
> money. You'd be writing a cheque to walk away from something you no longer own.
>
> There's your first answer. Forty months.
>
> And at the end of all of it — month seventy-two, thirty-six thousand three hundred and forty
> dollars gone — what you have is a six-year-old car worth about ten and a half thousand.

### ACT 4 — THE RE-HOOK (6:00–6:30)

> So: thirty-six thousand out, ten and a half thousand of car left.
>
> Now, some of that is just what cars are. Cars wear out; that's not a scandal, that's physics. I'm
> not going to pretend depreciation is a conspiracy.
>
> The eight thousand three hundred and forty is different. That one isn't physics. That one is a
> choice about how the paperwork was written — and that's the part I want to take apart. But first,
> the objection.

### ACT 5 — THE OBJECTION (6:30–8:00)

> "This is just what borrowing costs. Everyone pays for financing. If you don't like it, save up and
> pay cash."
>
> Fair. Let me give that its full weight, because there are three real things in it.
>
> **One:** paying cash genuinely is the cheapest option and always will be. If you can buy a car
> outright, do it, and this entire episode is a curiosity for you.
>
> **Two:** nine percent isn't everyone's rate. Good credit gets five or six. And zero percent
> promotional financing does exist — though it's usually funded by taking the cash-price discount
> away, so you often paid for it in the sticker instead. Run both offers to the total; that's the
> only comparison that means anything.
>
> **Three**, and this is the strongest version: money now really is worth more than money later.
> Someone gave up the use of twenty-eight thousand dollars for six years so you could drive
> immediately. That has a cost, and expecting it for free isn't reasonable.
>
> All true. And notice that none of it is the thing I'm objecting to.
>
> The problem isn't that the financier is paid. The problem is *what they're paid for*, and what
> happens to them when things go wrong. Because if you total that car in year two, the finance
> company's position doesn't change by a single dollar. They didn't own it. They never carried it.
> You do. And you keep paying.
>
> Everyone in that transaction took a risk on a physical object — except the one party earning the
> most from it.

### ACT 6 — THE TURN (8:00–8:40)

> So here's the question I promised. What did the lender actually sell you?
>
> Not the car — the dealer had the car, and the dealer got paid in full, in cash, on day one. Not
> risk — they hold a lien; if you stop paying they take the vehicle and you eat the loss. Not
> expertise, not maintenance, not insurance.
>
> They sold you **time.**
>
> Eight thousand three hundred and forty dollars, for the privilege of having the car in seventy-two
> monthly instalments instead of one payment. You didn't rent a car. You rented a calendar.
>
> And once you say it that plainly, it raises an obvious question: should time be a product? Because
> there's a legal tradition that spent a very long time on that question and concluded, firmly, that
> it shouldn't be.

### ACT 7 — THE DIVE (8:40–11:00)

> The rule in Islamic finance is short: **you may only profit from something you actually own and
> actually risk.**
>
> That single sentence eliminates the arrangement we just took apart. You cannot lend twenty-eight
> thousand dollars and require thirty-six thousand back, because the extra eight thousand is
> attached to nothing but the passage of time. That's *riba*. But — and this is the part people
> miss — you can absolutely still make a profit on a car. You just have to be in the deal.
>
> So the structure changes. Here's what it looks like.
>
> **Murabaha — cost-plus sale.** The financier doesn't lend you money. They go and *buy the car*.
> They take title. For however brief a period, that car is genuinely theirs, and it is genuinely
> their problem — if it's damaged before it reaches you, that's their loss. Then they sell it to you
> at a price that's fixed and disclosed up front: twenty-eight thousand becomes, say, thirty-one
> thousand five hundred, payable over six years. And here's the crucial part — **that number can
> never move again.** Not if you're late. Not if rates rise. Not if you take longer. There's no
> running clock, because there's no interest; there's a sale price and a payment schedule. A late
> payment can trigger a penalty, but in a properly structured contract that penalty goes to charity
> rather than to the financier's profit — precisely so nobody has an incentive to want you to fail.
>
> **Ijara — lease to own.** The financier buys the car and keeps owning it. You lease it, and
> because they're the owner, the ownership obligations are actually theirs: major maintenance,
> comprehensive insurance, and total loss. If the car is destroyed in year two, it was their asset.
> At the end of the term, ownership transfers to you.
>
> The difference between these and a conventional loan isn't the size of the payment. It's where the
> risk sits. In the conventional version, you carry the object and they carry nothing. In these, they
> have to stand behind the thing they're profiting from.
>
> **"Isn't this just interest with extra steps?"**
>
> I'd be wasting your time if I didn't take this seriously, because it's the most common criticism
> and it isn't stupid.
>
> Here's the honest position. Some murabaha in the real world *is* window dressing — where the
> financier owns the car for a legal instant, the markup is set by reading the same rate sheet a bank
> would use, and every economic feature is identical to a loan. Scholars argue about this constantly,
> and the ones who criticise it hardest are usually Islamic finance practitioners themselves. If your
> provider takes ownership for three seconds and prices off the base rate, you've bought paperwork.
>
> But the difference is real when the contract is real, and you can test it with three questions:
> **Does the total ever change if I'm late or slow?** **Who eats it if the car is destroyed before
> delivery?** **Is the markup fixed and disclosed in dollars, or expressed as a rate over time?**
>
> Fixed total, their risk, dollars not rates — that's a sale. Moving total, your risk, a rate — that's
> a loan wearing a costume, and you should say so.
>
> And the practical trade-off: expect a slightly higher headline total than the best conventional
> offer, and expect fewer providers. What you're buying is a price that cannot grow.

### ACT 8 — THE CLOSE (11:00–11:30)

> Twenty-eight thousand dollar car. Thirty-six thousand, three hundred and forty dollars paid.
> Forty months underwater. Eight thousand, three hundred and forty dollars for a vehicle that was
> never built.
>
> Before you sign anything: take the monthly payment, multiply by the number of months, and compare
> it to the sticker. That's the whole skill. It takes ten seconds and almost nobody does it.
>
> Next time: the contract where paying exactly what they ask, on time, every month, keeps you in
> debt for twenty-two years. And it isn't a mistake — it's the product.
>
> Run the numbers. Then run them halal.
