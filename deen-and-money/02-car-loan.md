## SCRIPT

### ACT 1 — MEET THEM (0:00–1:00)

> Meet Adam and Sami. Again.
>
> Both twenty-eight. Both earn fifty-six thousand dollars. Both drive a twelve-year-old car that
> starts most mornings and embarrasses them in the car park. Both have about five hundred dollars a
> month of genuine spare cash — and neither of them has a single dollar saved.
>
> And both of them want the exact same car. Same make, same model, same colour. Twenty-eight
> thousand dollars.
>
> Same age. Same income. Same car. Same five hundred a month. The only difference between these two
> men is **when.**
>
> Adam buys it on Saturday. Sami buys it two years later.
>
> Six years from now, that decision is worth about twenty-five thousand dollars — and I'll show you
> where every dollar of it comes from.

### ACT 2 — THE FORK (1:00–2:30)

> Adam goes in on Saturday morning and does the thing everyone does. He negotiates hard on the price
> — and he wins, genuinely, he gets a good deal on the car itself.
>
> Then he sits down in the small office off to the side, and in that room nobody mentions twenty-eight
> thousand dollars again. In that room there's one number: **five hundred and five dollars a month.**
>
> Seventy-two months. Nine percent. He signs, he gets the keys, and he drives home the same day.
>
> Sami wants the identical car and cannot afford it, and he decides to say that out loud instead of
> financing his way around it. So he does something that sounds simple and is socially quite
> difficult: he keeps the twelve-year-old car, and he moves five hundred and five dollars a month —
> exactly what Adam's payment is — into a savings account.
>
> He is not spending less than Adam. That's the whole point of this comparison. Both men have five
> hundred and five dollars a month leaving their account. Adam's goes to a finance company. Sami's
> goes into a box with his name on it.
>
> His plan is twenty-four months. Two years of driving the old car, and then he walks in and pays
> cash for a good used version of the same model.

### ACT 3 — MONTH 6 (2:30–3:40)

> Six months in, there is no comparison. Adam has won.
>
> He has the car. It's clean, it's quiet, it has a warranty, and it starts every single morning
> without the small prayer his old one required. He parks it at work where people can see it. Two
> colleagues have asked him about it. His mother mentioned it approvingly to someone.
>
> Sami is still driving the twelve-year-old car. In month four the alternator went and cost him three
> hundred and forty dollars. He's had to explain to two different people that no, he isn't getting a
> new one yet, and both times it landed somewhere between "sensible" and "struggling."
>
> He has three thousand and twenty-eight dollars in a savings account, which is not a thing anyone
> can see.
>
> One of these men has something. The other has a number.

### ACT 4 — CHECKPOINT: MONTH 24 (3:40–6:00)

> Two years. Let's compare.
>
> Adam has paid **twelve thousand, one hundred and thirteen dollars** into that loan.
>
> His remaining balance is **twenty thousand, two hundred and eighty dollars.**
>
> Twelve thousand paid, and the debt went down by seven thousand seven hundred.
>
> And now the number nobody looks at. The car — his actual car, the one in the driveway — is now
> worth about **seventeen thousand, three hundred and sixty dollars.**
>
> He owes twenty thousand two hundred and eighty. He owns something worth seventeen thousand three
> hundred and sixty.
>
> **Adam is two thousand, nine hundred and twenty dollars underwater.**
>
> If he sold the car today and handed the finance company every dollar he got for it, he would still
> have to write them a cheque for nearly three thousand dollars, to end up with no car at all. He
> has been in that position since about six weeks after he bought it, and he will stay in it until
> roughly month forty.
>
> Sami, this same week, walks into a dealership with **twelve thousand, one hundred and thirteen
> dollars** and buys a two-year-old version of Adam's car outright.
>
> Same model. Two years older, sixty percent of the price — because Adam already absorbed the
> steepest part of the depreciation for him.
>
> No payment. No balance. No interest. Sami owns a car worth twelve thousand dollars and owes
> nothing to anyone.
>
> And on that same day, his five hundred and five dollars a month keeps going out — except now it's
> going into an index fund instead of a savings account, because he no longer needs it liquid.

### ACT 5 — CHECKPOINT: MONTH 48 (6:00–8:00)

> Year four.
>
> Adam has now paid **twenty-four thousand, two hundred and twenty-seven dollars.** He owes eleven
> thousand and forty-eight. His car is worth about twelve thousand six hundred.
>
> He is finally above water. His equity in the car is **one thousand, five hundred and fifty-two
> dollars** — after four years and twenty-four thousand dollars.
>
> Sami has his car, now worth around nine thousand, and **twelve thousand, seven hundred dollars**
> invested.
>
> Here's the hidden cost that's been running underneath Adam's side the whole time and that I've
> deliberately not mentioned until now: **insurance.**
>
> Adam's finance agreement requires comprehensive cover on a new car — that's not optional, it's a
> condition of the loan, because the finance company is protecting its collateral. Sami, driving a
> twelve-year-old car for two years and a modest used one after, has been paying substantially less
> for the same driver, the same address, the same record. Over six years that difference alone runs
> to a few thousand dollars.
>
> The financed car doesn't just cost more to buy. It costs more to keep, and the loan is what
> requires it.

### ACT 6 — WAS IT WORTH IT? — NON-RESOLUTION (8:00–9:40)

> Now, I want to stop and put the other side properly, because if I don't, this is just a smug video
> about a man who waited.
>
> Adam got four years of a better car that Sami did not get.
>
> That is not nothing. Adam's car started every morning. It was safer — four years newer, better
> crash structure, better brakes. It had a warranty, so a failure was a phone call rather than a
> six-hundred-dollar decision. He drove his family in it. He never once stood at the side of a road.
>
> Sami spent two of those years in a car that failed twice, and one of those failures made him miss
> something that mattered. He also spent two years being quietly perceived as the guy who couldn't
> afford a car — and if you think that costs nothing, you've never been the only person at a wedding
> parking two streets away.
>
> So: is roughly twenty-five thousand dollars worth four years of a nicer, safer, more reliable car?
>
> I genuinely don't think that's an obvious no. For a family with small children and a long commute,
> reliability isn't a luxury and I'm not going to pretend otherwise.
>
> What I'd say is this. If Adam had done that arithmetic — if he had sat down and said *this
> reliability is worth eight thousand three hundred and forty dollars in finance costs to me, and I
> choose it* — then Adam made a real decision and I have nothing to argue with.
>
> He didn't. Nobody does. That number was never presented to him at any point in the transaction,
> and that's the actual problem. Not that he financed a car. That he financed a car without ever
> being shown the price of financing it.

### ACT 7 — THE HINGE (9:40–10:40)

> So let's put that price on the table, and ask what it bought.
>
> Eight thousand, three hundred and forty dollars.
>
> Not the car — the dealer sold him the car, and the dealer was paid in full, in cash, on day one.
> Not maintenance, not insurance, not risk: if Adam had written that car off in year two, the
> finance company's position wouldn't have moved by a dollar. They never owned it. Adam would still
> owe every cent.
>
> They sold him **time.** Eight thousand three hundred and forty dollars for the right to have the
> car in seventy-two instalments instead of one payment. He didn't rent a car. He rented a calendar.
>
> Everybody in that transaction carried a risk on a physical object — the manufacturer, the dealer,
> Adam himself — except the one party earning the most from it.

### ACT 8 — CHECKPOINT: MONTH 72 (10:40–12:00)

> Six years. Final numbers.
>
> Adam has made his last payment. Total paid: **thirty-six thousand, three hundred and forty dollars.**
> He owns a six-year-old car worth about **ten thousand, five hundred.**
>
> Sami's total spend on transport across the same six years: **twelve thousand dollars.** He owns a
> car worth about seven thousand. And his five hundred and five a month, invested from month
> twenty-five onward, is now **twenty-eight thousand, four hundred and forty-one dollars.**
>
> **Adam: ten thousand five hundred. Sami: thirty-five thousand, four hundred and forty-one.**
>
> **Twenty-four thousand, nine hundred and forty-one dollars apart.**
>
> And notice the shape of it, because it's not all interest. Eight thousand three hundred is the
> finance cost. The rest is that Adam bought a brand-new car and paid the depreciation on the first
> two years — the steepest part — while Sami let a stranger absorb it and bought the same vehicle
> afterwards. The interest is the visible half of the mistake. The timing is the invisible half, and
> it's bigger.

### ACT 9 — THE OBJECTION (12:00–13:00)

> "Not everyone can wait two years. Some people need a reliable car *now* — for work, for children,
> for a job that doesn't exist without one."
>
> Completely true, and it's the reason this comparison isn't a moral one.
>
> If you cannot get to work without a reliable car, financing one is not a failure of discipline —
> it's a rational purchase of income. A car that lets you keep a job is worth more than eight
> thousand dollars of finance cost, easily.
>
> Second concession: Sami's plan needed a working car to bridge two years. Someone whose old car is
> already dead doesn't have Sami's option at all, and telling them to wait is telling them to lose
> their job.
>
> What survives all of that: almost nobody in Adam's position was choosing between financing and
> unemployment. Adam had a car that started. He upgraded, on credit, because the payment fit and the
> total was never mentioned. That's the common case, and it's the one worth eight thousand dollars.

### ACT 10 — THE THIRD MAN (13:00–14:00)

> There was a third man.
>
> Bilal needed the car on Saturday, same as Adam. He couldn't wait two years and he didn't pretend
> he could. But he wasn't willing to rent a calendar.
>
> So the structure changed. His financier didn't lend him money — **they bought the car.** They took
> title to it. For that period it was genuinely theirs, and genuinely their problem: if it had been
> damaged before delivery, that's their loss, not his.
>
> Then they sold it to him at a price fixed and disclosed up front. Twenty-eight thousand became
> **thirty-one thousand five hundred**, payable over six years — and that number **can never move
> again.** Not if he's late, not if rates rise, not if he takes longer. There's no interest clock,
> because there's no interest; there's a sale price and a schedule. This is **murabaha** — a
> cost-plus sale — and the rule underneath it is that you may only profit from something you actually
> owned and actually risked.
>
> His payment is four hundred and thirty-seven fifty. Total paid: **thirty-one thousand five hundred.**
> **Four thousand, eight hundred and forty dollars less than Adam**, for the same car on the same day.
> And the sixty-seven dollars a month he isn't handing over is worth another six thousand two hundred
> invested by month seventy-two.
>
> Bilal finishes at about **sixteen thousand, seven hundred.** Well behind Sami — waiting still beat
> everything — but nearly six thousand ahead of Adam, with the car in his driveway the whole time.
>
> One honest warning. Some murabaha in the real world is window dressing: the financier owns the car
> for a legal instant and prices the markup straight off the same rate sheet a bank would use. Three
> questions tell you which one you've got. **Does the total change if I'm late? Who eats it if the
> car is destroyed before delivery? Is the markup fixed in dollars, or expressed as a rate over time?**
> Fixed total, their risk, dollars not rates — that's a sale. Anything else is a loan in a costume.

### ACT 11 — CLOSE (14:00–14:10)

> Sami won because he waited. Bilal won because he changed the contract. Adam lost because nobody
> ever showed him the total.
>
> Before you sign anything: monthly payment, times number of months. Ten seconds. Almost nobody does it.
>
> Next time: two men owe five thousand dollars on the same credit card, at the same rate. One is debt
> free in three years. The other is still paying in sixteen.
>
> Run the numbers. Then run them halal.
