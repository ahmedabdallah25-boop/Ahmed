One paragraph per scene, in scene order. Lines 6 and 15 end on an
em-dash and carry straight into the next line — read them through, no pause.

TAG NOTES: 25 of 46 lines carry a tag — serious 7, emphatic 7, calm 6, warmly 3, sad 1, whispers 1.
Tags are sparse on purpose: on a 46-line read, a tag on every line
flattens into no tags at all. They sit on the turns — the crossover at the
19/20 cut, Adam under the block at 24, the comply/default flip at 25, the
write-off payoff at 31, and the close at 45. Never two tags on one line.

[serious] You have paid this every month for six years.

[emphatic] You have never touched it.

The number is bigger now than the day you walked out.

[calm] So look at what you actually signed.

They handed you a sum.

You agreed to hand back a slice of what you earn —

but only the part above a line.

Earn under the line, you pay nothing.

Over it, a percentage.

[serious] Plan Two. Income-driven. Deferment.

[emphatic] Same machine.

[warmly] And most people stop reading right there. Because that does sound fair.

[calm] It would be. If the number sat still while you paid it.

[emphatic] It doesn't.

Every single day, interest is added —

not to the part you haven't paid yet, but to the whole outstanding balance.

[serious] Which means there are two flows here, not one.

Money leaving you every month. A slice of your salary, capped by law.

[emphatic] And money being added every month. A percentage of everything you
owe, uncapped, growing with the balance itself.

[calm] Now hold those two side by side.

Somewhere there is an income where they are exactly equal. Call it the
crossover.

[serious] Earn above it, and your payment eats the interest and starts on the
balance. The debt falls.

[emphatic] Earn below it, and the balance does not fall slowly. It does not fall
at all. It rises. While you pay it.

[sad] Ten years of payments, never missed, and you owe more than the day you
started.

That is not what happens when you default. [emphatic] That is what happens when
you comply.

And notice which way it cuts.

The graduate who earns well crosses over, clears it, and stops paying.

[serious] The graduate who earns less pays every month for thirty years and
never clears anything.

Same debt. The one with less money pays longer, and pays more.

Then, decades in, the balance is written off.

[emphatic] Which sounds like mercy. It isn't. It's the pricing.

The lender's own forecasts already assume a large share of borrowers will never
clear the balance.

The write-off isn't the escape hatch at the end of the machine. It is a line in
the machine's own budget.

[calm] And this is exactly what the prohibition is pointing at.

Riba isn't a tax on borrowing. It is a price charged for time.

A number that grows because the clock moved — not because anything was built,
sold, or risked.

[serious] A debt you service faithfully and cannot reduce is the purest version
of that there is.

[warmly] So here is the two-minute check.

[calm] One. Find two published numbers: your interest rate, and your repayment
threshold.

Two. Work out one month. Interest added, against payment taken.

[serious] If the interest is bigger, you are renting the debt, not repaying it.

Three. Only then decide about overpaying.

Above the crossover it genuinely shortens it. Far below, and written off anyway,
it is money burned. Same arithmetic, both directions.

[calm] And for the ones coming behind you: qard hasan funds, employer tuition
schemes, paying as you study. Smaller, slower, less convenient. Also not this.

[whispers] You didn't fail to pay it off. You were never paying it off.

[warmly] The Money Machine, Decoded.

ACCURACY NOTES — every claim that must hold
================================================================================

MUST HOLD:
1. Interest accrues on the whole outstanding balance, not on an unpaid portion.
   True of UK Plan 2/Plan 5 and of US federal unsubsidised loans.
2. Required repayment is a percentage of income above a threshold, with nothing
   owed below it. True of UK income-contingent repayment and of US
   income-driven repayment plans.
3. Negative amortisation — balance rising while payments are made — follows
   arithmetically from 1 and 2 whenever accrued interest exceeds the required
   payment. This is the video's central claim and it is a matter of arithmetic,
   not of jurisdiction.
4. Balances are written off/forgiven after a set period. UK Plan 2 is 30 years
   from entering repayment; Plan 5 is 40. US income-driven forgiveness is
   typically 20-25 years.
5. Government forecasting assumes a substantial share of borrowers never repay
   in full. The UK models this explicitly as a resource-accounting cost.

DELIBERATELY WEAKENED, AND WHY:
- NO interest rate, threshold figure, or repayment percentage is stated. All
  three change annually and differ by market; naming them dates the video
  within a year and breaks the "name no product" rule. The script says "a
  slice" and "a percentage" and sends the viewer to their own two published
  numbers instead. This is also what makes the pack a reusable template.
- "Ten years" and "thirty years" are illustrative of the shape, not a claim
  about any specific borrower's schedule.
- US plans are referred to generically as "income-driven". Individual plan names
  are omitted: the plan landscape has been unusually unstable and any named plan
  risks being wrong by the time this is watched.
- Forgiven balances can be taxable in some US cases. Not mentioned — it is a
  real complication but a jurisdictional one, and including it would fracture
  the universal frame the whole pack is built on. Flagged here so the omission
  is a decision on the record rather than an oversight.
- Scholars differ on borrowing under necessity (darura) where no alternative
  route to education exists. The script does not issue a verdict on the
  individual — it explains the mechanism and hands over the check. That is the
  channel's position and it should stay that way.

DESCRIPTION
You have paid it every month for six years. You have never touched it.

Here is the mechanism nobody explains. You agreed to repay a slice of what you
earn above a threshold — capped, predictable, and it does sound fair. But
interest is charged every day on the WHOLE outstanding balance, and that charge
is uncapped, because it grows with the balance itself.

So there are two flows, not one. Money leaving you, capped. Money joining the
debt, uncapped.

Somewhere between them is a crossover — an income where the two are exactly
equal. Above it, your payment eats the interest and starts on the balance, and
the debt falls. Below it, the balance does not fall slowly. It does not fall at
all. It rises, while you pay it, faithfully, for a decade.

That is not what happens when you default. It is what happens when you comply.

And it cuts the wrong way. The graduate who earns well crosses over, clears the
debt and stops paying. The graduate who earns less pays for thirty years and
clears nothing. Same debt — the one with less money pays longer, and pays more.
Then it is written off, which sounds like mercy. It isn't. The lender's own
forecasts already assume most of that money is never coming back. The write-off
isn't the escape hatch at the end of the machine; it is a line inside the
machine's budget.

Plan Two. Income-driven. Deferment. Same machine.

THE TWO-MINUTE CHECK:

1. Find two published numbers — your interest rate and your repayment threshold.
   Both are on your statement or your servicer's site.
2. Work out a single month. Interest added, against payment taken. If the
   interest is bigger, you are renting the debt, not repaying it.
3. Only then decide about overpaying. Above the crossover it genuinely shortens
   the debt. Far below it, with a write-off coming anyway, it is money burned.
   Same arithmetic, run honestly, in both directions.

Riba is not a tax on borrowing. It is a price charged for time — a number that
grows because the clock moved, not because anything was built, sold or risked.
A debt you service faithfully and cannot reduce is the purest form of it there
is.

You didn't fail to pay it off. You were never paying it off.

The Money Machine, Decoded.
